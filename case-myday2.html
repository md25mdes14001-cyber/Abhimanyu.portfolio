The Problem

Productivity apps are guilt machines

Traditional to-do apps assume you have unlimited energy. They let you pile on tasks with no awareness of your actual capacity — cognitive load, sleep quality, emotional state, or just how human you feel today. The result? Chronic overplanning and underperformance, which breeds shame.

The deeper problem isn't lack of tools — it's a design philosophy that treats productivity as a fixed resource rather than a variable, living one. myday² was designed to challenge that assumption at its root.

Through 40+ user interviews, a recurring pattern emerged: people weren't failing at their tasks. They were failing at estimating themselves.

78%users abandon to-do apps within 3 weeks
40+user interviews conducted

Core Insight

Plan around capacity, not just time

Instead of asking "what do you need to do today?", myday² opens with a single question: "How much can you give today?" This reframe is deceptively powerful. It signals to the user that their system is on their side — not judging them, not pushing them, just helping them do what they can, well.

Behavioral Trigger

Daily capacity check-in becomes a ritual, not a chore — anchoring realistic intention before the day starts.

Agentic Planning

AI reads your capacity score, open tasks, calendar blocks, and energy patterns to generate a realistic day plan — not a wish list.

Adaptive Tasks

When priorities shift mid-day, the system re-plans in real time. Tasks adjust, not the person.

Calm Feedback

End-of-day journaling and weekly pattern insights replace guilt-inducing completion percentages with honest reflection.

"What if your productivity app helped you do less, and feel better about it?" — Core design question that shaped myday²
The Solution

Six features. One philosophy.

Every feature in myday² flows from the same principle: human capacity is variable, and a good system works with it, not against it. Calm technology — in the tradition of Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown — means the app stays in the periphery until you need it, and never creates more cognitive load than it relieves.

🔋

Capacity-First Planning

Daily check-in anchors your plan to your real energy level. The system never asks you to promise more than you have.

🤖

AI-Powered Day Plan

The agentic planner synthesises your tasks, calendar, and capacity score into a realistic, prioritised day schedule — automatically.

🔄

Adaptive Tasks

If something changes — a meeting overruns, energy dips — the plan adapts in real time. No manual reshuffling required.

📅

Calendar Integration

Reads your existing calendar to understand what time is truly free, so plans are always grounded in reality, not wishful thinking.

📓

Daily Journal

End-of-day prompts guide a two-minute reflection. No streaks, no shame — just honest check-ins that build self-knowledge over time.

📊

Insights & Patterns

Weekly pattern recognition surfaces your peak energy windows, common blockers, and capacity trends — so planning gets smarter week by week.

Design Process

From insight to shipped feature

The design followed a double-diamond process accelerated by AI-assisted research synthesis. Each stage had a clear output, ensuring the team never confused activity with progress.

01

Empathize

40+ user interviews, diary studies, and competitive teardowns of 12 productivity apps.

02

Define

Capacity paradox identified as the core problem. "Guilt loop" pattern documented across personas.

03

Ideate

80+ concepts sketched across 3 design sprints. Calm technology principles applied as filters.

04

Design

6-screen core flow: check-in → AI plan → focused work → adaptive updates → journal → insights.

05

Test

3 rounds of usability testing with 24 participants. Guilt-spiral rate: 0% in final prototype.

06

Launch

MVP shipped with capacity check-in + AI day plan. 3× task completion vs. baseline apps in pilot.

Outcomes

What changed when capacity came first

Pilot participants used myday² for 4 weeks alongside their previous productivity system. The results were less about doing more, and more about feeling right-sized — which turned out to be far more sustainable.
